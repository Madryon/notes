1. Amorphous vs Crystalline Solids
Concept ⌄
Definition & Key Differences
Crystalline solids: atoms arranged in a perfectly regular, periodic 3D pattern. Exhibit long-range order. Examples: NaCl, diamond, quartz, metals.
Amorphous solids: atoms arranged randomly, no long-range periodicity. Only short-range order exists. Examples: glass, rubber, wax, plastics.

2. Lattice, Basis & Unit Cell
Core ⌄
Lattice
A crystal lattice is an infinite array of points in 3D space, where every point has an identical environment. It describes the geometric arrangement.
Lattice translation vectors: a, b, c are three non-coplanar vectors. Any lattice point is reached by: R = n₁a + n₂b + n₃c where n₁, n₂, n₃ are integers.
Basis
A basis is the group of atoms (or molecules) associated with each lattice point.
Key relation: Crystal Structure = Lattice + Basis
Example: NaCl lattice is FCC, with basis = one Na⁺ + one Cl⁻ ion.
Unit Cell
The unit cell is the smallest volume that, when repeated by translation, generates the entire crystal.
Primitive unit cell: contains exactly 1 lattice point. Volume = a·(b×c).
Non-primitive cell: contains more than 1 lattice point (BCC=2, FCC=4).
Wigner-Seitz cell: primitive cell formed by drawing perpendicular bisectors between a lattice point and all its neighbors.
Bravais Lattices
There are 14 Bravais lattices in 3D, grouped under 7 crystal systems: Cubic, Tetragonal, Orthorhombic, Monoclinic, Triclinic, Trigonal, Hexagonal.
Cubic system has 3 lattices: SC (Simple Cubic), BCC, FCC.

3. Miller Indices
Exam Favourite ⌄
Definition
Miller indices (hkl) are a set of integers used to describe the orientation of crystal planes and directions.
Steps to Find Miller Indices
Step-by-step Method (write this in exam)
Find where the plane intercepts the three crystal axes: x-intercept = p, y-intercept = q, z-intercept = r (in units of a, b, c).
Take the reciprocals: 1/p, 1/q, 1/r.
Clear fractions by multiplying with the LCM to get three smallest integers.
Enclose in parentheses: (hkl). Negative value → put bar on top: (h̄kl).
Special Cases
Plane parallel to an axis → intercept = ∞ → Miller index = 0.
(100): plane cuts x-axis, parallel to y and z.
(110): plane cuts x and y, parallel to z.
(111): plane cuts all three axes equally.
{hkl} denotes a family of planes; [hkl] denotes a direction; ⟨hkl⟩ denotes a family of directions.
Interplanar Spacing (Cubic System)
d_hkl = a / √(h² + k² + l²)
where a = lattice parameter, (h, k, l) = Miller indices
Numerical problems: given a plane that cuts axes at (2, 3, 1) → reciprocals = (1/2, 1/3, 1) → LCM=6 → (3, 2, 6). Practice 5–6 such problems before the exam.
4. Reciprocal Lattice
Core ⌄
Concept
The reciprocal lattice is a mathematical construct in k-space (momentum space) derived from the direct lattice.
Every point in the reciprocal lattice corresponds to a set of parallel planes (hkl) in the direct lattice.
Distance of reciprocal lattice point from origin = 1/d_hkl.
Reciprocal Lattice Vectors
a* = 2π(b × c) / V
b* = 2π(c × a) / V
c* = 2π(a × b) / V

where V = a·(b × c) = volume of unit cell

Orthogonality: a·a* = 2π, a·b* = 0, a·c* = 0
Properties
The reciprocal of the reciprocal lattice is the original direct lattice.
BCC direct lattice ↔ FCC reciprocal lattice, and vice versa.
Diffraction condition: Δk = G (where G is a reciprocal lattice vector). This is the Laue condition.
5. Brillouin Zones & Ewald Construction
Important ⌄
Brillouin Zones
A Brillouin zone is the Wigner-Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice.
First Brillouin zone: region in k-space closer to the origin (k=0) than to any other reciprocal lattice point.
Constructed by drawing perpendicular bisector planes to lines connecting origin to nearest reciprocal lattice points.
At Brillouin zone boundaries: Bragg reflection occurs → standing waves form → energy gaps open.
All allowed electron states (k values) can be mapped into the first BZ (reduced zone scheme).
Ewald's Construction (Geometrical Approach)
A geometrical method to find which crystal planes satisfy the diffraction condition.
Steps of Ewald Construction
Draw the reciprocal lattice of the crystal.
Draw the incident wave vector k₀ ending at a reciprocal lattice point (the origin O).
Draw a sphere (Ewald sphere) of radius |k₀| = 1/λ centered at the tail of k₀.
Wherever this sphere passes through another reciprocal lattice point G, diffraction occurs in the direction k = k₀ + G.
Condition for diffraction: |k₀| = |k| (elastic scattering), so the diffracted beam vector must also terminate on the Ewald sphere.
Draw the Ewald sphere diagram clearly with k₀, k, and G labeled. Show the reciprocal lattice points. This is a guaranteed diagram question.
6. X-ray Diffraction & Bragg's Law
Exam Favourite ⌄
Why X-rays?
X-ray wavelength (0.1–10 Å) is comparable to interatomic distances in crystals → diffraction occurs.
Visible light (thousands of Å) passes through without diffracting on crystal planes.
Bragg's Law Derivation (brief)
Consider two parallel crystal planes separated by distance d. X-rays strike at glancing angle θ.
Ray reflected from second plane travels extra path = 2d sinθ.
Constructive interference when: extra path = nλ.
Bragg's Law
2d sinθ = nλ

n = order of diffraction (1, 2, 3...)
d = interplanar spacing (Å)
θ = glancing angle (angle between beam and plane surface)
λ = wavelength of X-rays

Note: θ in Bragg's law is glancing angle, NOT angle of incidence from normal.
Applications of X-ray Diffraction
Determination of crystal structure and lattice parameters.
Identification of unknown crystalline materials (fingerprinting).
Measurement of interplanar spacings.
Study of protein and DNA structures (Rosalind Franklin's DNA X-ray images).
How to Solve Bragg's Law Numericals
Identify given quantities: wavelength λ, glancing angle θ or d.
Note the order n (usually n=1 if not stated).
Apply: 2d sinθ = nλ and solve for the unknown.
For d: use d = a/√(h²+k²+l²) if Miller indices are given.
Bragg's law numericals are very common. Common mistakes: (1) using angle from normal instead of glancing angle — always check. (2) Forgetting the factor of 2 in 2d sinθ.

1. Lattice Vibrations & Phonons
Concept ⌄
Lattice Vibrations
Atoms in a crystal are not static — they continuously vibrate about their equilibrium positions.
These vibrations are coupled: motion of one atom affects neighbors. This creates collective wave-like modes called normal modes.
For a crystal with N unit cells and p atoms per cell: total 3pN normal modes exist.
Phonons
A phonon is a quantized unit of lattice vibrational energy, analogous to how photon is a quantum of electromagnetic energy.
Phonons are bosons (integer spin); their number is not conserved.
Phonon Properties
Energy: E = ℏω = hν
Momentum: p = ℏk (crystal momentum)
Velocity: v_g = dω/dk (group velocity)
Acoustic phonons: at long wavelengths, atoms move in-phase (like sound waves). ω → 0 as k → 0.
Optical phonons: atoms of different types move out-of-phase. ω ≠ 0 at k = 0. Can interact with light (hence "optical").
2. Linear Monoatomic Chain
Key Formula ⌄
Model
1D chain of N identical atoms, each of mass m, connected by springs of constant C, equilibrium spacing = a.
Only nearest-neighbor interactions considered.
Equation of motion: m(d²uₙ/dt²) = C(uₙ₊₁ + uₙ₋₁ − 2uₙ)
Dispersion Relation
Dispersion Relation (Monoatomic Chain)
ω² = (4C/m) sin²(ka/2)
or: ω = 2√(C/m) |sin(ka/2)|

Maximum frequency: ω_max = 2√(C/m) at k = ±π/a (zone boundary)
First Brillouin zone: −π/a ≤ k ≤ +π/a
Features
At small k (long wavelength): ω ≈ a√(C/m)·k → linear dispersion → behaves like sound waves.
Speed of sound: v = a√(C/m).
Only one branch (acoustic) since one atom per unit cell.
ω is periodic in k with period 2π/a — all distinct modes lie within first BZ.
3. Linear Diatomic Chain
Exam Favourite ⌄
Model
1D chain with two alternating masses M and m (M > m), spring constant C, unit cell spacing = 2a (contains both atoms).
Two Branches
Dispersion Relation (Diatomic Chain)
ω² = C(1/M + 1/m) ± C√[(1/M + 1/m)² − (4sin²ka)/(Mm)]

(+) sign → Optical branch (upper)
(−) sign → Acoustic branch (lower)
Key Values
BranchAt k=0At k=π/2a (zone boundary)
Acousticω = 0ω = √(2C/M)
Opticalω = √(2C(M+m)/Mm)ω = √(2C/m)
Frequency Gap
A frequency gap (forbidden band) exists between ω = √(2C/M) and ω = √(2C/m).
Phonons with frequencies in this range cannot propagate through the crystal.
In acoustic branch: both atoms move in the same direction. In optical branch: atoms move in opposite directions.

4. Heat Capacity — Dulong-Petit, Einstein & Debye
Important ⌄
Dulong-Petit Law (Classical)
Each atom treated as a 3D harmonic oscillator: 3 kinetic + 3 potential = 6 degrees of freedom.
Energy per atom = 6 × (½k_BT) = 3k_BT. For one mole: E = 3Nk_BT = 3RT.
Dulong-Petit Law
Cv = 3Nk_B = 3R ≈ 24.9 J/mol·K (temperature independent)
Failure: observed Cv decreases significantly at low temperatures → classical theory fails.
Einstein Model (1907)
Einstein assumed all N atoms vibrate independently at the same frequency ωE (Einstein frequency).
At high T: Cv → 3Nk_B (recovers Dulong-Petit). At low T: Cv → 0 exponentially.
Einstein Heat Capacity
Cv = 3Nk_B (θE/T)² · e^(θE/T) / [e^(θE/T) − 1]²

Einstein temperature: θE = ℏωE / k_B
At high T: Cv → 3Nk_B ✓
At low T: Cv → 3Nk_B(θE/T)²e^(−θE/T) (drops too fast — limitation)
Limitation: drops too steeply at low T; observed Cv follows T³, not exponential decay.
Debye Model (1912)
Debye treated the crystal as an elastic continuum with a spectrum of frequencies from 0 to a maximum ωD (Debye cutoff frequency).
Density of states: g(ω) ∝ ω² (parabolic). Total number of modes = 3N.
Debye Heat Capacity
At high T: Cv → 3Nk_B (Dulong-Petit recovered)

At low T (T << θD): Cv = (12π⁴/5) Nk_B (T/θD)³ ← Debye T³ law

Debye temperature: θD = ℏωD / k_B
Example values: θD(Pb) = 105K, θD(Fe) = 470K, θD(diamond) = 2230K
ModelAssumptionLow-T behaviorAccuracy
Dulong-PetitClassical; 6 DOF per atomConstant (wrong)Only high T
EinsteinSingle frequency ωEExponential decayQualitative only
DebyeSpectrum 0 to ωDT³ law (correct!)Best overall
Always explain why Dulong-Petit fails, why Einstein is better but not perfect, and why Debye's T³ law matches experiments. This comparison is a full question by itself.

1. Kronig-Penney Model & Band Formation
Important ⌄
The Model
The Kronig-Penney model is a simplified 1D model of a crystal where the periodic potential is approximated as a series of rectangular potential wells and barriers (square wells).
An electron experiences a periodic potential: V(x) = V(x + a), where a is the lattice constant.
The Schrödinger equation is solved in two regions: (1) well region (potential = 0), (2) barrier region (potential = V₀).
Key Result (Qualitative)
The solution allows electron propagation only for certain ranges of energy (allowed bands).
Other energy ranges are forbidden (band gaps / energy gaps).
At zone boundaries (k = nπ/a), Bragg reflection creates standing waves → energy gaps open.
The parameter P = mV₀ba/ℏ² (where b = barrier width) controls gap width: larger P → wider gaps.
Limiting Cases
P → 0 (no barriers): free electrons, no gaps, continuous E-k curve (parabola).
P → ∞ (infinite barriers): isolated atoms, sharp energy levels only.
Real crystals: intermediate P → alternating allowed bands and forbidden gaps.
Classification of Solids

ConductorPartially filled band OR overlapping bandsZeroCu, Al, Fe
SemiconductorFilled valence + empty conduction~0.1–3 eVSi (1.1 eV), Ge
InsulatorFilled valence + empty conduction>3 eVDiamond (5.5 eV)

2. Effective Mass
Concept ⌄
Why Effective Mass?
In a crystal, an electron is subject to both the external applied force AND the periodic potential of the lattice.
We absorb the lattice effect into a modified mass — the effective mass m* — so the equation of motion looks like Newton's law for a free particle.
Effective Mass Definition
m* = ℏ² / (d²E/dk²)

F_external = m* × a (equation of motion)

Near band minimum (bottom): d²E/dk² > 0 → m* > 0 (electron-like)
Near band maximum (top): d²E/dk² < 0 → m* < 0 (hole-like)
Physical Meaning
m* can be much smaller than free electron mass (high curvature) → fast electron response.
m* can be negative at band top → "holes" in the valence band behave as positive charge carriers.
For a free electron: E = ℏ²k²/2m → d²E/dk² = ℏ²/m → m* = m (recovers free electron result).
3. Hall Effect
Exam Favourite ⌄
Phenomenon
When a current-carrying conductor/semiconductor is placed in a magnetic field perpendicular to the current, a voltage develops in the third perpendicular direction. This is the Hall effect (1879, E.H. Hall).
Setup
Current I flows in +x direction. Magnetic field B is applied in +z direction.
Lorentz force on charge carriers: F = qv × B acts in −y direction for electrons (for holes, +y).
Carriers accumulate on one face → buildup of transverse electric field E_H (Hall field).
Equilibrium: Hall force qE_H balances Lorentz force qv_xB → steady state Hall voltage.
Hall Effect Formulas
Hall voltage: V_H = IB / (ned)
Hall field: E_H = V_H / w = R_H × J × B
Hall coefficient (electrons): R_H = −1/(ne) < 0
Hall coefficient (holes): R_H = +1/(pe) > 0

Carrier mobility: μ = |R_H| × σ = |R_H| / ρ

where n = electron density, p = hole density, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C
Applications of Hall Effect
1. Type of carrier: sign of V_H tells whether electrons (n-type) or holes (p-type) carry current.
2. Carrier concentration: n or p can be calculated from R_H.
3. Carrier mobility: μ = R_H × σ.
4. Magnetic field measurement: Hall probes measure magnetic field strength.
5. Current sensors: contactless current measurement using Hall sensors.
How to write Hall Effect answer
State the phenomenon in 2 lines with setup (current, field, voltage directions).
Draw the diagram showing current direction, B field, and Hall voltage polarity for both n-type and p-type.
Write the force balance equation leading to Hall field.
State the formula for V_H and R_H.
List 4–5 applications in bullet points.
4. Introduction to Superconductivity
Concept ⌄
Definition
Below a critical temperature Tc, certain materials exhibit zero electrical resistance → this is superconductivity (discovered by Kamerlingh Onnes, 1911 in mercury at 4.2 K).
Properties
Zero resistance: resistivity drops sharply to zero below Tc. Persistent currents flow without decay.
Meissner effect: a superconductor completely expels magnetic field from its interior (perfect diamagnetism, B = 0 inside). This distinguishes superconductors from merely perfect conductors.
Critical field Hc: superconductivity is destroyed when applied field exceeds Hc.
Types
Type I: complete Meissner effect up to Hc, then sudden transition to normal. Example: Pb, Hg, Al.
Type II: two critical fields Hc1 and Hc2. Below Hc1: Meissner. Hc1 to Hc2: mixed state (vortex state). Above Hc2: normal. Example: Nb, YBCO.
BCS Theory (Qualitative)
Proposed by Bardeen, Cooper, Schrieffer in 1957 (Nobel Prize 1972).
Electrons form Cooper pairs via phonon-mediated attractive interaction (one electron distorts lattice → attracts another electron).
Cooper pairs condense into a single quantum ground state → no scattering → zero resistance.
Applications
MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) machines, particle accelerator magnets (LHC), SQUID magnetometers, Maglev trains, fault current limiters, quantum computers.

2. Langevin Theory of Dia & Paramagnetism
Important ⌄
Langevin Theory of Diamagnetism
Mechanism: an applied magnetic field causes a change in orbital velocity of electrons → induced magnetic moment opposes the applied field (Lenz's law applied to electron orbits).
This effect is present in ALL materials, but in para/ferromagnetics it is overwhelmed.
Diamagnetic susceptibility is temperature-independent (no thermal randomization involved).
Langevin Diamagnetic Susceptibility
χ_dia = −Ne²⟨r²⟩ / (6m_ec²) [Gaussian]
χ_dia = −μ₀Ne²⟨r²⟩ / (6m_e) [SI]

Always negative (opposes field), temperature independent.
Langevin Theory of Paramagnetism
Atoms have permanent magnetic dipole moments (due to unpaired electron spins).
Without field: thermal agitation randomizes directions → net M = 0.
With field: moments partially align along field. Alignment vs thermal randomization → equilibrium.
Langevin used classical statistics: M = Nμ·L(x), where L(x) = coth(x) − 1/x is the Langevin function, x = μB/k_BT.
Paramagnetic Susceptibility — Curie's Law
For small x (high T, weak B): M ≈ Nμ²B / (3k_BT)

χ = M/H = μ₀Nμ² / (3k_BT) = C/T ← Curie's Law

Curie constant: C = μ₀Nμ² / (3k_B)

χ decreases with T — agrees with experiments!
For the exam: State the classical assumption, write M = Nμ L(x), explain the high-T limit to get Curie's law. Always state that Curie's Law χ = C/T means susceptibility decreases as temperature rises.
3. Weiss Theory of Ferromagnetism & Domains
Important ⌄
Weiss Molecular Field Theory
Weiss (1907) proposed that in ferromagnets, each magnetic dipole experiences an enormous "molecular field" (Weiss field) due to its neighbors, in addition to the external field.
Weiss field: B_w = λM, where λ is the Weiss molecular field constant (very large ≈ 10³ T).
Total effective field: B_eff = B_applied + λM.
This strong internal field aligns all moments → spontaneous magnetization even without external field.
Curie-Weiss Law (above Tc)
χ = C / (T − θ) where θ = λC = Tc (Curie temperature)

At T = Tc: susceptibility → ∞ (transition to ferromagnetic state)
Below Tc: ferromagnetic (spontaneous order)
Above Tc: paramagnetic (thermal disorder wins)

Tc(Fe) = 1043 K, Tc(Ni) = 631 K, Tc(Co) = 1388 K
Ferromagnetic Domains
A ferromagnetic crystal is divided into small regions called domains, each having all dipoles aligned in one direction.
Different domains point in different directions → total magnetization of unmagnetized material = 0.
Domain boundaries are called Bloch walls (gradual rotation of moments).
In an external field: domains aligned with field grow (domain wall motion) → material magnetizes.
Domains minimize total energy (magnetostatic energy + exchange energy + anisotropy energy).
Draw a diagram showing a ferromagnet with multiple domains pointing in different directions. Then show how applying a field causes favorable domains to grow. This diagram scores well.
4. B-H Curve, Hysteresis & Energy Loss
Exam Favourite ⌄
Magnetization Curve (B-H Curve)
Start with demagnetized material (B = 0, H = 0).
As H increases: B rises along initial magnetization curve (OA), reaching saturation Bs.
When H is reduced back to 0: B does not fall to 0 → residual flux density = retentivity Br.
H must be reversed (coercive force −Hc) to bring B to 0.
Further increasing H in negative direction → reverse saturation.
Completing the cycle traces a closed loop — the hysteresis loop.
Key Points on the Loop
PointNameMeaning
BsSaturation flux densityMax B, all domains aligned
BrRetentivity / RemanenceB remaining when H = 0
HcCoercive forceReverse H needed to demagnetize
Energy Loss
Energy dissipated per cycle = area enclosed by the hysteresis loop (converted to heat).
Power loss = (area of loop) × (volume) × (frequency).
Hard vs Soft Magnetic Materials

How to draw B-H hysteresis curve in exam
Draw x-axis as H, y-axis as B. Mark origin O.
Draw initial magnetization curve from O to saturation point S (top right).
As H→0, curve drops to Br on y-axis (above origin).
Curve continues to −Hc on x-axis (left of origin).
Goes to −Bs (bottom left), then Br on negative y-axis, then +Hc, then back to +Bs.
Label ALL 5 key points: Bs, Br, Hc, −Br, −Hc. Shade the area = energy loss.

Dielectric Materials

1. Polarization, Susceptibility & Polarizability
Key Formulas ⌄
Key Quantities
Polarization P: electric dipole moment per unit volume. Units: C/m². P = Np (N = number density, p = individual dipole moment).
Electric susceptibility χe: measures how easily a dielectric is polarized. P = ε₀χeE.
Relative permittivity (dielectric constant) εr: εr = 1 + χe. Always ≥ 1.
Polarizability α: microscopic quantity; p = αE_local (dipole moment of individual atom/molecule in local field).
Fundamental Dielectric Relations
D = ε₀E + P = ε₀εrE
P = ε₀(εr − 1)E = ε₀χeE
εr = 1 + χe
p = αE_local (per atom)
Types of Polarization
Electronic polarization: displacement of electron cloud relative to nucleus. Present in ALL materials. Very fast (optical frequencies).
Ionic polarization: displacement of positive and negative ions in ionic crystals (NaCl). Present in ionic materials.
Orientational (dipolar) polarization: rotation of permanent dipoles in polar molecules (H₂O, HCl). Frequency-dependent; slows at high frequency.
Space charge polarization: accumulation of charges at interfaces in inhomogeneous materials. Only at very low frequencies.
Know the frequency dependence: at high frequency, only electronic polarization contributes (εr(optical) < εr(static)). This distinction often appears in exams.
2. Local Electric Field & Clausius-Mossotti Equation
Exam Favourite ⌄
Local Electric Field
The local field E_local experienced by an individual atom in a solid ≠ the macroscopic applied field E. The surrounding dipoles create an additional field.
Lorentz local field calculation: Consider a spherical cavity around the atom; contributions from (1) external field, (2) depolarization, (3) Lorentz cavity, (4) atoms inside cavity.
For a cubic crystal: E_local = E + P/(3ε₀) (Lorentz relation)
Clausius-Mossotti Equation
Relates the macroscopic measurable quantity εr to the microscopic quantity α (polarizability).
Derivation uses P = Nα·E_local with Lorentz field E_local = E + P/(3ε₀) and P = ε₀(εr−1)E.
Clausius-Mossotti Equation
(εr − 1) / (εr + 2) = Nα / (3ε₀)

For optical frequencies (n = refractive index, εr = n²):
(n² − 1) / (n² + 2) = Nα / (3ε₀) ← Lorentz-Lorenz equation

Rearranged to find εr:
εr = (1 + 2Nα/3ε₀) / (1 − Nα/3ε₀)
Significance
Bridges microscopic (atomic polarizability α) and macroscopic (dielectric constant εr) descriptions.
Catastophe: if Nα = 3ε₀, then εr → ∞ — this corresponds to a ferroelectric transition!
Useful for predicting dielectric constants from atomic data and vice versa.
Exam writing strategy
State why local field ≠ applied field (surrounding dipoles contribute).
Write the Lorentz local field expression: E_local = E + P/3ε₀.
Write P = Nα·E_local, substitute E_local, and use P = ε₀(εr−1)E.
After algebra, arrive at the Clausius-Mossotti equation and state what it relates (εr to α).
3. Ferroelectricity & P-E Hysteresis Loop
Important ⌄
Ferroelectricity
Ferroelectric materials exhibit spontaneous electric polarization that can be reversed by an external electric field.
Examples: BaTiO₃ (barium titanate), Rochelle salt (NaKC₄H₄O₆·4H₂O), PZT, KH₂PO₄ (KDP).
Below Curie temperature Tc: ferroelectric phase (spontaneous polarization exists).
Above Tc: paraelectric phase (no spontaneous polarization). χ = C/(T − Tc) (Curie-Weiss law).
Ferroelectric Domains
Like ferromagnets, ferroelectrics have domains of uniform spontaneous polarization.
Domain walls are thin regions where polarization rotates from one domain direction to another.
P-E Hysteresis Loop
Applying an electric field E and measuring polarization P gives a hysteresis loop (analogous to B-H loop).
Saturation polarization Ps: maximum P when all domains are aligned.
Remnant polarization Pr: P remaining when E is reduced to zero.
Coercive field Ec: reverse E needed to bring P to zero.

(ii) Define primitive and non-primitive unit cell of a 2D oblique lattice [3 marks]
Primitive Unit Cell
A primitive unit cell is the smallest possible unit cell that contains exactly one lattice point and generates the entire lattice by repetitive translation alone.
It has the minimum volume (or area in 2D). The primitive cell of a 2D oblique lattice is a parallelogram formed by the two primitive vectors a and b.
Area of 2D primitive cell = |a × b| = ab sin φ, where φ is the angle between a and b.
Non-Primitive Unit Cell
A non-primitive unit cell contains more than one lattice point (2, 4, etc.) and has a volume that is an integer multiple of the primitive cell volume.
It is chosen for convenience when it better reflects the symmetry of the crystal (e.g., the conventional unit cells of BCC and FCC).

(iii) Explain the concept of local electric field in a dielectric material [3 marks]
What is Local Electric Field?
The local electric field (E_local) is the actual electric field experienced by an individual atom or molecule inside a dielectric, at its own site.
It is not equal to the macroscopic (average) applied field E because the atom is surrounded by other polarized atoms that create their own fields.
Lorentz Method for Local Field
Lorentz imagined cutting a small spherical cavity around the atom in question. The local field has contributions from:
1. External applied field E₀
2. Depolarization field E₁: due to charges on outer surface of the specimen (−P/3ε₀ for sphere).
3. Lorentz cavity field E₂: due to surface charges on the imaginary spherical cavity (+P/3ε₀).
4. Field E₃: due to atoms inside the cavity (= 0 for cubic symmetry).
Lorentz Local Field
E_local = E + P/(3ε₀)
where E = macroscopic field, P = polarization
Since P/(3ε₀) > 0, the local field is always greater than the macroscopic field, meaning atoms are polarized more than the average field suggests.

(v) How can Hall effect identify n-type and p-type semiconductors? [3 marks]
Principle
When a semiconductor carrying current I (in +x direction) is placed in magnetic field B (in +z direction), a transverse Hall voltage V_H develops (in y direction). The sign of V_H identifies the carrier type.
For n-type (electrons are majority carriers)
Electrons flow in −x direction (current is +x). Lorentz force F = −e(v × B) deflects electrons towards −y face.
Negative charge accumulates on the −y face → Hall voltage is negative (V_H < 0).
Hall coefficient: R_H = −1/ne < 0 (negative for n-type).
For p-type (holes are majority carriers)
Holes flow in +x direction. Lorentz force deflects holes towards −y face.
Positive charge accumulates on −y face → Hall voltage is positive (V_H > 0).
Hall coefficient: R_H = +1/pe > 0 (positive for p-type).
Hall Coefficient
R_H = E_y / (J_x · B) = −1/(ne) for n-type
R_H = +1/(pe) for p-type

Carrier density: n or p = 1/(|R_H|·e)

(vi) What are ferromagnetic domains? Give examples of ferromagnetic materials. [3 marks]
Ferromagnetic Domains
A ferromagnetic domain is a small microscopic region within a ferromagnetic material in which all the atomic magnetic moments are aligned parallel to one another in the same direction.
Each domain has a net magnetic moment, but different domains point in different directions, so the material as a whole may appear unmagnetized (net M = 0).
Domain boundaries are called Bloch walls — thin transition regions (100–1000 atoms thick) where the magnetic moment gradually rotates from one domain direction to the next.
When an external magnetic field is applied, domains aligned with the field grow at the expense of others (domain wall motion) → the material magnetizes.
Above the Curie temperature (Tc), thermal energy destroys domain order → material becomes paramagnetic.
Ferromagnetic Domains (Unmagnetized → Magnetized)
Examples of Ferromagnetic Materials
Iron (Fe) — Tc = 1043 K, most common ferromagnet.
Nickel (Ni) — Tc = 631 K, used in magnets and coins.
Cobalt (Co) — Tc = 1388 K (highest among common ferromagnets).
Gadolinium (Gd) — ferromagnetic near room temperature (Tc ≈ 292 K).
Alnico, neodymium magnets (Nd₂Fe₁₄B) — alloy/compound ferromagnets used in industry.

BCC Primitive Vectors
The primitive vectors of the BCC lattice (with conventional cell side a) are:
BCC Primitive Vectors
a₁ = (a/2)(−x̂ + ŷ + ẑ)
a₂ = (a/2)(x̂ − ŷ + ẑ)
a₃ = (a/2)(x̂ + ŷ − ẑ)
Volume of BCC Primitive Cell
V = a₁ · (a₂ × a₃)

First, a₂ × a₃ = (a/2)²[(x̂−ŷ+ẑ) × (x̂+ŷ−ẑ)]
= (a²/4)[−x̂(−1−1) − ŷ(−1−1) + ẑ(1+1)] ... (expanding)
= (a²/4)[2x̂ + 2ŷ + 2ẑ] = (a²/2)(x̂+ŷ+ẑ)

V = (a/2)(−1+1+1) × (a²/2) = a³/2
Reciprocal Lattice Vectors of BCC
Using b* = 2π(aⱼ × aₖ)/V
b₁ = 2π(a₂ × a₃)/V = 2π·(a²/2)(x̂+ŷ+ẑ) / (a³/2)
= (2π/a)(x̂ + ŷ + ẑ) ... (1)

b₂ = 2π(a₃ × a₁)/V = (2π/a)(x̂ + ŷ − ẑ) ... (2) [similar calculation]

b₃ = 2π(a₁ × a₂)/V = (2π/a)(−x̂ + ŷ + ẑ) ... (3)
Identifying the Reciprocal Lattice
These three vectors b₁, b₂, b₃ are identical in form to the primitive vectors of an FCC lattice with lattice parameter a' = 4π/a (i.e., a conventional FCC with side 4π/a).
The primitive vectors of FCC are of the form: (a'/2)(ŷ+ẑ), (a'/2)(x̂+ẑ), (a'/2)(x̂+ŷ) — same structure as b₁, b₂, b₃ above.
Conclusion
∴ The reciprocal lattice of BCC is FCC (with lattice parameter 4π/a).
Similarly, the reciprocal of FCC is BCC. These two lattices are reciprocals of each other.
(b) Write Bragg's condition for crystal diffraction using Ewald's construction [6 marks]
Bragg's Condition in Reciprocal Space (Laue Condition)
If k is the incident wave vector and k' is the scattered wave vector, then for elastic scattering: |k| = |k'| = 2π/λ.
Diffraction occurs when the change in wave vector equals a reciprocal lattice vector G:
Laue / Bragg Diffraction Condition
k' − k = G (Laue condition)
or equivalently: Δk = G

where G = hb₁ + kb₂ + lb₃ is a reciprocal lattice vector (hkl = Miller indices)
Ewald's Construction — Steps
1. Plot the reciprocal lattice of the crystal on paper.
2. Choose a reciprocal lattice point as the origin O. Draw the incident wave vector k ending at O (so k starts at point C).
3. Draw a sphere of radius |k| = 2π/λ centered at C. This is the Ewald sphere.
4. Wherever the Ewald sphere passes through another reciprocal lattice point G, the vector from C to G is the diffracted wave vector k' = k + G.
5. The diffraction condition k' − k = G is satisfied, and a diffracted beam exits in the direction of k'.
Ewald's Construction
Connection to Bragg's Law
The Laue condition k' − k = G, combined with |k| = |k'|, is completely equivalent to Bragg's law 2d sinθ = nλ. Both describe the same physical condition from different perspectives (real space vs. reciprocal space).

The Kronig-Penney Model
The Kronig-Penney model is a simplified one-dimensional model of an electron in a periodic crystal potential.
The periodic potential is approximated as a series of rectangular potential wells (representing atom cores) separated by rectangular barriers (potential V₀, width b). The period is a+b.
Kronig-Penney Periodic Potential
Salient Features / Key Results
1. The solutions to Schrödinger's equation give a condition on allowed energies. Only certain ranges of energy (bands) are permitted; others are forbidden (gaps).
2. The bandwidth depends on parameter P = mV₀ba/ℏ²: larger P → narrower bands, wider gaps.
3. At zone boundaries (k = ±nπ/a), energy gaps open due to Bragg reflection of electron waves.
4. Limiting case P→0: free electrons (parabolic E-k, no gaps). P→∞: isolated atoms (discrete levels).

(b) Obtain the mathematical relation for effective mass of electron in solids [6 marks]
Concept of Effective Mass
In a crystal, an electron is subject to the periodic potential of the lattice in addition to any external force. The effect of the lattice is absorbed into a modified mass — the effective mass m*.
Derivation
Step 1: Consider an electron in a band with energy E(k). The group velocity of the electron wave packet is:
v_g = (1/ℏ)(dE/dk) — group velocity
Step 2: Apply external force F. From Newton's second law: F = dp/dt = ℏ(dk/dt).
Step 3: Differentiate v_g with respect to time:
dv_g/dt = (1/ℏ)(d²E/dk²)(dk/dt)
= (1/ℏ)(d²E/dk²)(F/ℏ)
= F · (1/ℏ²)(d²E/dk²)
Step 4: Compare with Newton's law: F = m*(dv_g/dt):
Effective Mass Formula
m* = F / (dv_g/dt) = ℏ² / (d²E/dk²)

∴ m* = ℏ² / (d²E/dk²)
Physical Interpretation
m* depends on the curvature of the E-k band. High curvature (rapidly curving band) → small m* → electron responds quickly to forces.
Near band minimum: d²E/dk² > 0 → m* > 0 (electron-like behavior).
Near band maximum: d²E/dk² < 0 → m* < 0 (hole-like behavior — positive charge carrier).
For free electrons: E = ℏ²k²/2m → d²E/dk² = ℏ²/m → m* = m (reduces to free electron mass ✓).
(c) What is superconductivity? Give an example of a superconductor. [3 marks]
Superconductivity
Superconductivity is the phenomenon in which the electrical resistivity of certain materials drops abruptly to zero when cooled below a critical temperature T_c (the transition/Curie temperature).
Discovered by H. Kamerlingh Onnes (1911) in mercury at T_c = 4.2 K.
Key Characteristics
1. Zero resistance: below T_c, resistivity ρ = 0. Currents flow indefinitely without decay.
2. Meissner effect: superconductor expels all magnetic flux from its interior (perfect diamagnetic, B = 0 inside).
3. BCS Theory: electrons form "Cooper pairs" via phonon-mediated attraction; these pairs condense into one quantum state with no scattering.
Example
Mercury (Hg): T_c = 4.2 K (first discovered). Niobium (Nb): T_c = 9.2 K. YBCO (YBa₂Cu₃O₇): T_c = 92 K (high-temperature superconductor, used in MRI machines).

(b) Derive Dulong and Petit's law; explain Einstein and Debye improvements [7 marks]
Dulong-Petit Law (Classical Derivation)
Consider N atoms in a crystal. Each atom vibrates in 3D → modeled as 3D harmonic oscillator.
Each harmonic oscillator has 2 degrees of freedom per dimension (kinetic + potential), so 6 DOF per atom.
By the Equipartition Theorem: energy per DOF = ½k_BT.
Dulong-Petit Derivation
Energy per atom = 6 × ½k_BT = 3k_BT
Total energy of N atoms: U = 3Nk_BT
Specific heat: Cv = dU/dT = 3Nk_B

For 1 mole (N = N_A): Cv = 3N_Ak_B = 3R ≈ 24.9 J/mol·K

This is the Dulong-Petit law — Cv is constant, independent of temperature.
Experimental failure: At low temperatures, Cv decreases drastically and approaches 0 as T→0 K. Classical theory cannot explain this.
Einstein's Theory (1907)
Einstein applied quantum mechanics: each atom is a quantum harmonic oscillator vibrating at a single frequency ωE (Einstein frequency).
Average energy per oscillator: ⟨E⟩ = ℏωE/(e^{ℏωE/k_BT} − 1) + ½ℏωE.
Einstein Heat Capacity
Cv = 3Nk_B(θE/T)² · e^{θE/T}/(e^{θE/T} − 1)²
Einstein temperature: θE = ℏωE/k_B

High T limit: Cv → 3Nk_B ✓ (Dulong-Petit recovered)
Low T limit: Cv ∝ e^{−θE/T} (drops exponentially — but real Cv drops as T³)
Limitation: Einstein model drops too steeply at low T; real solids follow T³ not exponential at low T.
Debye's Theory (1912)
Debye treated the crystal as an elastic continuum. Phonon frequencies range from 0 to a maximum Debye frequency ωD, with density of states g(ω) ∝ ω².
Debye cutoff: ∫g(ω)dω = 3N (total modes).
Debye Result
High T: Cv → 3Nk_B ✓
Low T (T << θD): Cv = (12π⁴/5)Nk_B(T/θD)³ ← Debye T³ law

Debye temperature: θD = ℏωD/k_B
θD(Fe) = 470 K, θD(Al) = 428 K, θD(Pb) = 105 K
Superiority: Debye's T³ law matches experiments at low temperatures. Debye model is the most accurate classical-quantum theory of lattice heat capacity.

Q5 Langevin Diamagnetism, B-H Loop with Domains & Polarizability Types [6, 6, 3] ⌄
(a) Derive Langevin's theory of diamagnetism; show susceptibility is negative and temperature-independent [6 marks]
Basic Idea
Diamagnetism arises from the change in orbital velocity of electrons when an external magnetic field is applied. By Lenz's law, the induced current opposes the applied field → susceptibility is negative.
Derivation
Step 1: Consider an electron of charge −e, mass m, moving in a circular orbit of radius r with angular velocity ω₀. The centripetal force is provided by the Coulomb attraction:
mω₀²r = e²/(4πε₀r²) → mω₀²r = F₀ ... (1)
Step 2: Apply magnetic field B perpendicular to the orbital plane. An additional Lorentz force acts on the electron: F_L = evB = e(ω'r)B.
Step 3: New equation of motion (new angular velocity ω'):
mω'²r = F₀ ± eω'rB (+ for one sense, − for opposite)

m(ω'² − ω₀²)r = ∓eω'rB
m(ω' + ω₀)(ω' − ω₀) ≈ ∓eω'B (since ω' ≈ ω₀)

Change in angular velocity: Δω = ω' − ω₀ = ∓eB/(2m)
Step 4: This Δω is the Larmor precession frequency. The change in magnetic moment per electron:
Δμ = −e(Δω)r²/2 = −e²r²B/(4m) (per electron, per dimension)
Step 5: For Z electrons per atom, using ⟨r²⟩ = ⟨x²+y²⟩ = (2/3)⟨ρ²⟩ (3D average):
Diamagnetic Susceptibility
Total Δμ per atom = −Ze²⟨r²⟩B/(6m)

Magnetization: M = N·Δμ = −NZe²⟨r²⟩B/(6m)

χ_dia = μ₀M/B = −μ₀NZe²⟨r²⟩/(6m)
Conclusions
1. χ is negative: The formula has a negative sign → magnetization opposes applied field → diamagnetic. ✓
2. χ is temperature-independent: The expression contains no temperature T. The orbital radius and Larmor precession are independent of thermal motion. ✓
This means diamagnetic susceptibility is the same at all temperatures — confirmed experimentally.
Final Result
χ_dia = −μ₀NZe²⟨r²⟩/(6m) = constant (negative, T-independent) ✓
(b) Draw B-H hysteresis loop for ferromagnet; explain with domain theory [6 marks]
B-H Hysteresis Loop
B-H Hysteresis Loop — Ferromagnetic Material
Explanation Using Domain Theory
Segment O → Bs (initial curve): Starting from demagnetized state, as H increases, domains aligned with H begin to grow by domain wall movement. Favorable domains expand, unfavorable domains shrink. At saturation Bs, all domains are aligned with H.
Bs → Br (H reduced to 0): When H is removed, domain walls do not return to original positions (pinned by defects/impurities). Some domains remain aligned → residual magnetism Br (retentivity).
Br → −Hc (reverse field applied): A reverse field H is needed to demagnetize the material. At coercive force Hc, enough domains have switched → B = 0.
−Hc → −Bs: Further increasing reverse H aligns all domains in the reverse direction → negative saturation −Bs.
−Bs → −Br → +Hc → +Bs: Completing the cycle in the forward direction traces the bottom half of the loop symmetrically.
Energy Loss
The area enclosed by the hysteresis loop represents the energy dissipated as heat per unit volume per cycle. This is called hysteresis loss.
Energy loss/cycle = ∮H·dB × Volume
Soft ferromagnets (narrow loop): low Hc, small area → low energy loss → used in transformer cores and electromagnets.
Hard ferromagnets (wide loop): large Hc, large Br → high energy loss but retain magnetism → used as permanent magnets.
(c) Define electronic, ionic and orientational dipolar polarizability for dielectric materials [3 marks]
1. Electronic Polarizability (α_e)
When an electric field is applied, the electron cloud of an atom is displaced relative to the positive nucleus, creating an induced dipole moment.
Present in all materials (atoms, ions, and molecules).
Very fast response — remains effective even at optical frequencies (~10¹⁵ Hz).
p_e = α_e · E   →  α_e = 4πε₀R³ (for sphere of radius R)
2. Ionic Polarizability (α_i)
In ionic crystals (e.g., NaCl), an applied field causes relative displacement of positive and negative ion sub-lattices, creating a net dipole moment per unit cell.
Slower than electronic polarization; effective up to infrared frequencies (~10¹² Hz).
Absent in covalent crystals (like diamond) where there are no separate ions.
p_i = α_i · E   where α_i depends on ionic masses and restoring force constants
3. Orientational (Dipolar) Polarizability (α_o)
Polar molecules (e.g., H₂O, HCl) have a permanent electric dipole moment. Without a field, these are randomly oriented (net P = 0).
An applied field tends to align these permanent dipoles, contributing to polarization. Thermal agitation opposes perfect alignment.
Slowest response — only at low frequencies (below microwave, ~10⁹ Hz). Absent in non-polar materials.
p_o = α_o · E   where α_o = μ²/(3k_BT) (Langevin result, T-dependent)
