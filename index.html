UNIT I Introduction to Research Methodology 6 hrs
1.1   Scientific method — history & meaning ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

The scientific method is a systematic, logical procedure for acquiring knowledge through observation, hypothesis formation, experimentation, and conclusion. It originated with Greek philosophers like Aristotle but was formalised during the Scientific Revolution (16th–17th century) by Francis Bacon (inductive reasoning), René Descartes (deductive reasoning), and Galileo Galilei (experimental verification). The method is self-correcting — knowledge is continuously revised based on new evidence. The key steps are: (1) Observation, (2) Problem identification, (3) Hypothesis formulation, (4) Experimentation, (5) Data analysis, and (6) Conclusion. Research is a systematic investigation to establish facts, solve problems, or build new knowledge. The objectives of research include: gaining familiarity with phenomena, describing characteristics, determining frequency, testing hypotheses, and establishing causal relationships.

Core concept
The scientific method is a loop, not a one-time process. Each cycle refines knowledge. Bacon's inductive approach = observe many cases → generalise. Descartes' deductive approach = start with theory → test specific predictions.
Galileo dropped balls of different masses from the Leaning Tower of Pisa — observing that both landed simultaneously, disproving Aristotle's claim that heavier objects fall faster. This is the experimental verification step in action.
Types of research
Basic research — builds theory (no immediate application). Applied research — solves specific practical problems. Descriptive — describes what exists. Analytical — examines cause-effect. Qualitative — non-numerical, interpretive. Quantitative — numerical, statistical.
Basic: studying dark matter properties. Applied: designing a better MRI scanner using those properties.
  • Scientific method steps: Observation → Hypothesis → Experiment → Analysis → Conclusion
  • Francis Bacon: Father of inductive scientific method (observe → generalise)
  • Galileo: Introduced experimental verification as a cornerstone
  • Research objectives: Familiarity, description, hypothesis testing, causal relations Often asked
  • Basic vs Applied: Basic = theory, no immediate use; Applied = solves real problems
  • Qualitative tenets: Naturalistic, inductive, holistic, subjective, context-sensitive
1.2   Research problem — identification & formulation ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

A research problem is a specific gap, contradiction, or unanswered question in existing knowledge that motivates a scientific investigation. The process of formulation involves: (1) Identifying a broad area of interest, (2) Conducting a preliminary literature review, (3) Narrowing the problem to a specific researchable question, (4) Operationally defining variables, and (5) Stating the problem precisely and unambiguously.

Sources of research problems: personal experience, contradictions in existing literature, social needs, expert suggestions, and gaps revealed in previous research.

Bias and prejudice are systematic distortions in research. Types include: Personal bias (researcher's own values influencing results), Sampling bias (non-representative sample), Measurement bias (faulty instruments), and Observer bias (expectations affecting recording). These are minimised through random sampling, double-blind designs, pre-registration, and calibrated instruments.

What makes a good research problem?
A research problem must be: Feasible (doable with available resources), Interesting (worth investigating), Novel (fills a gap), Ethical, and Relevant (FINER criteria).
Poor problem: "Study climate change." (Too broad) Better: "Does increasing PM2.5 concentration in Delhi winters correlate with a rise in paediatric asthma cases in 2019–2023?"
Types of bias
Bias is systematic — it consistently pushes results in one direction, unlike random error which averages out. The key distinction examiners test: bias ≠ random error.
Sampling bias: surveying only urban students about internet access will overestimate connectivity rates nationwide.
  • Research problem: Specific gap or unanswered question in existing knowledge
  • Steps to formulate: Broad area → Literature review → Narrow → Operationalise → State precisely
  • Sources: Personal experience, expert suggestion, gaps in literature, social needs
  • Personal bias: Researcher's values distort data interpretation
  • Sampling bias: Non-representative sample → wrong conclusions
  • Measurement bias: Faulty/uncalibrated instrument → systematic error
  • Observer bias: Expectation affects how data is recorded Fix: blind studies
1.3   Literature review — tools & methods ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

A literature review is a critical, comprehensive survey of existing published work on a research topic. It identifies what is known, reveals gaps, provides a theoretical framework, and justifies the methodology. It is not a summary list — it is a thematic synthesis.

Open-source (free) tools: Google Scholar, PubMed, arXiv, DOAJ (Directory of Open Access Journals), Semantic Scholar, SSRN, Unpaywall (accesses paywalled papers legally).

Paid tools: Web of Science, Scopus, JSTOR, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, Nature, and Springer.

Reference management software: Zotero (free), Mendeley (free), EndNote (paid), JabRef (free, for LaTeX). These tools store citations, generate bibliographies, and track reading status.

Primary sources are original research papers and raw data. Secondary sources are review articles, textbooks, and meta-analyses that summarise primary sources.

Why review literature before starting research?
Without a literature review, you might unknowingly duplicate existing work, use an outdated methodology, or miss the most relevant theoretical framework for your study.
A physics student wanting to study superconductivity at room temperature would first search ADS and arXiv to see what materials have already been tested — avoiding repeating failed experiments.
Systematic vs Narrative review
Systematic review: Structured, reproducible, pre-defined criteria for including/excluding papers. Gold standard in medical research. Narrative review: Author-driven, selective, interpretive. Common in humanities and early-stage research.
A systematic review on "efficacy of hydroxychloroquine for COVID-19" would define exact search terms, databases, and inclusion criteria before starting — making it replicable.
  • Literature review purpose: Find gaps, avoid duplication, build framework, justify method
  • Free tools: Google Scholar, arXiv, PubMed, DOAJ, Unpaywall, Semantic Scholar
  • Paid tools: Web of Science, Scopus, JSTOR, ScienceDirect, IEEE Xplore
  • Reference managers: Zotero (free), Mendeley (free), EndNote (paid), JabRef (LaTeX) Name all 4
  • Primary source: Original research paper, raw experimental data
  • Secondary source: Review articles, textbooks, meta-analyses
  • Systematic review: Structured, reproducible — gold standard
  • Narrative review: Selective, author-driven interpretation
UNIT II Data Collection, Analysis & Interpretation 15 hrs
2.1   Methods of data collection ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

Data can be collected using five major methods:

1. Survey: Structured questionnaire administered to a large group. Generates quantitative data quickly and cheaply. Limitation: cannot probe deeper meanings; susceptible to response bias.
2. Interview: Direct researcher-participant conversation. Can be structured (fixed questions), semi-structured (flexible), or unstructured (open-ended). Generates rich qualitative data. Limitation: time-consuming, interviewer bias.
3. Observation: Researcher watches subjects in natural (field observation) or controlled (lab) settings. Can be participant (researcher joins the group) or non-participant. Limitation: observer effect (Hawthorne effect — people behave differently when watched).
4. Experimentation: Manipulates an independent variable (IV) and measures its effect on a dependent variable (DV) while keeping all other variables (confounds) constant. Most powerful method for establishing causality.
5. Case study: In-depth investigation of a single subject, event, or organisation. Yields rich detail. Limitation: low external validity — findings cannot be generalised.

When to use which method?
The choice depends on what you want to know. Use surveys when you need numbers from many people. Use interviews when you need depth and nuance. Use experiments when you need to prove causality.
Survey: "What % of students own a laptop?" — needs 500+ responses, survey is ideal. Interview: "How do students feel about online exams?" — needs opinions and experiences, interview is better.
Hawthorne effect
When people know they are being observed, they change their behaviour — usually becoming more productive or behaving better. This was discovered in studies at the Hawthorne Works factory in the 1920s. It is a major limitation of observational research.
Workers in a factory produced more when observed by researchers — not because of any change in working conditions, but simply because they were being watched.
  • Survey: Questionnaire, large sample, quantitative, cheap, but surface-level
  • Interview: Structured / semi-structured / unstructured; qualitative; deep but slow
  • Observation: Participant vs non-participant; Hawthorne effect is key limitation
  • Experimentation: IV → DV, control confounds; best for causality Define IV and DV
  • Case study: Single subject, deep detail, low generalisability
  • Likert scale: 1–5 or 1–7 scale used in surveys (Strongly Disagree → Strongly Agree)
2.2   Descriptive statistics — central tendency & dispersion ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

Descriptive statistics summarise and describe the main features of a dataset without drawing conclusions beyond the data itself.

Measures of Central Tendency:
— Mean: Arithmetic average = (Sum of all values) / n. Best for symmetric distributions. Sensitive to outliers.
— Median: Middle value when data is arranged in order. Robust to outliers. Best for skewed data.
— Mode: Most frequently occurring value. Used for categorical data.

Measures of Dispersion:
— Range: Maximum − Minimum. Simple but sensitive to outliers.
— Standard Deviation (σ): Average deviation of each data point from the mean.

σ = √[ Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / n ]

A low σ means data is tightly clustered around the mean (precise). A high σ means data is widely spread (variable). In physics: low σ = high precision; accuracy (closeness to true value) is separate — improved by calibration, not averaging.

When to use mean vs median?
Always ask: does this dataset have outliers or is it skewed? If yes → use median. If data is symmetric → mean and median are close, use either.
Dataset: 2, 3, 4, 5, 100. Mean = 22.8 (pulled up by 100). Median = 4. The median better represents the "typical" value here.
Skewness and central tendency
Right skew (positive): Mean > Median > Mode (tail on the right — income data, exam scores). Left skew (negative): Mode > Median > Mean (tail on the left — age at death). Symmetric: Mean = Median = Mode.
Indian household income data is right-skewed: a few billionaires pull the mean upward, while most households earn far less than the mean — the median is a better policy measure.
  • Mean: Σx / n — use for symmetric data, affected by outliers
  • Median: Middle value — use for skewed data, robust to outliers
  • Mode: Most frequent — use for categorical data
  • Range: Max − Min — simple, crude, outlier-sensitive
  • Standard deviation σ: √[Σ(xᵢ−x̄)²/n] — best measure of spread Write formula
  • Variance: σ² — in squared units, harder to interpret than SD
  • Precision vs accuracy: Precision = low SD; Accuracy = closeness to true value
  • Right skew: Mean > Median (income, exam scores)
2.3   Hypothesis testing — Z-test, T-test, Type I & II errors ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

Hypothesis testing is a statistical procedure to decide whether to reject the null hypothesis (H₀) based on sample data.

H₀ (Null hypothesis): States no effect or no difference exists. The default position.
H₁ (Alternative hypothesis): States an effect exists. Accepted if H₀ is rejected.

Z-test: Used when sample size n > 30 AND population standard deviation (σ) is known.

Z = (x̄ − μ) / (σ / √n)

T-test: Used when n ≤ 30 OR σ is unknown. Three types: one-sample, independent two-sample, paired t-test.

t = (x̄ − μ) / (s / √n), df = n − 1

Type I error (α): Rejecting H₀ when it is true — false positive. Probability = α (typically 0.05).
Type II error (β): Failing to reject H₀ when it is false — false negative. Probability = β.
Statistical power = 1 − β = probability of correctly detecting a real effect.
Trade-off: Decreasing α increases β. Increasing sample size reduces both simultaneously.

Z-test vs T-test — the decision rule
Think of it this way: when you don't know the population SD (which is almost always the case in practice), you use the T-test. The t-distribution has heavier tails than the normal distribution — it accounts for the extra uncertainty from estimating σ from the sample. As n grows, the t-distribution converges to the normal distribution.
Testing if a new teaching method changes scores: 25 students, population SD unknown → use t-test. Census data with 10,000 entries, known σ → use Z-test.
Type I and Type II errors — real life
Think of a COVID test. Type I error: test says "positive" but person is healthy (false alarm — triggers unnecessary quarantine). Type II error: test says "negative" but person is infected (missed case — person spreads disease).
In drug approval: Type I = approving a useless drug (harm from side effects with no benefit). Type II = rejecting an effective drug (patients miss out on a cure). Regulators choose α = 0.05 to limit Type I errors.
  • H₀: No effect/difference — researcher tries to disprove it
  • H₁: Effect exists — accepted if H₀ is rejected
  • Z-test: n > 30, σ known; formula Z = (x̄−μ)/(σ/√n)
  • T-test: n ≤ 30 or σ unknown; df = n−1 3 types
  • 3 types of T-test: One-sample, Independent two-sample, Paired
  • Type I (α): Reject true H₀ — false positive — probability = α
  • Type II (β): Accept false H₀ — false negative — probability = β
  • Power: 1 − β — ability to detect a real effect
  • Degrees of freedom: df = n − 1 for one-sample t-test
  • Mnemonic: Type I = crying wolf; Type II = missing the wolf
2.4   Regression analysis & curve fitting ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

Regression analysis models the relationship between a dependent variable (Y) and one or more independent variables (X).

Simple linear regression:

Y = β₀ + β₁X + ε

where β₀ = intercept, β₁ = slope (change in Y per unit X), ε = residual error.
Multiple linear regression:

Y = β₀ + β₁X₁ + β₂X₂ + … + βₙXₙ + ε

R² (coefficient of determination): Proportion of variance in Y explained by the model. R² = 0.85 means 85% of variation in Y is explained.

Nonlinear curve fitting: When data does not follow a straight line, iterative methods are used.
— Gradient descent (steepest descent): Moves in the direction of steepest decrease of the error function. Slow near the minimum.
— Marquardt (Levenberg-Marquardt) method: A hybrid of gradient descent and Gauss-Newton. Behaves like gradient descent far from the minimum, and like Gauss-Newton (faster) near it. It is the gold standard for nonlinear least-squares fitting.

What does the regression coefficient tell you?
β₁ = 3 means: for every 1-unit increase in X, Y increases by 3 units on average. β₀ is the value of Y when X = 0. The residual ε is the gap between the actual Y and the Y predicted by the model.
Y = exam score, X = hours studied. β₁ = 5 means: each extra hour of study raises the expected score by 5 marks. R² = 0.72 means studying hours explain 72% of score variation.
Marquardt vs gradient descent
Gradient descent is like walking downhill blindly — slow but reliable. Gauss-Newton is like taking a mathematical shortcut — fast but only works if you are already close to the bottom. Marquardt switches between the two intelligently.
Fitting a radioactive decay curve A·e^(−λt) to data from a gamma ray detector uses the Marquardt method — it handles the non-linearity robustly even with a poor initial guess.
  • Linear regression: Y = β₀ + β₁X + ε; β₁ = slope, β₀ = intercept
  • Multiple regression: Multiple X variables predict Y
  • R²: Proportion of variance explained (0 to 1; closer to 1 = better fit)
  • Assumptions: Linearity, independence, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals
  • Gradient descent: Iterative, moves toward error minimum, slow near solution
  • Marquardt method: Hybrid — gold standard for nonlinear fitting Must know
  • Residual (ε): Actual Y − Predicted Y; should be random with zero mean
  • Calibration: Reduces systematic error; comparison against known standard
  • Error propagation: For z = x+y → Δz = √(Δx² + Δy²)
UNIT III Journals, Databases & Research Metrics 7 hrs
3.1   Types of journals — peer-review, OA, predatory ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

Journals are broadly classified as:

1. Subscription-based (paid) journals: Access requires institutional or personal subscription. Examples: Nature, Science, Physical Review (APS).

2. Open Access (OA) journals: Free to read online. Gold OA — author pays Article Processing Charge (APC), free for readers. Green OA — author self-archives a version in a repository (arXiv, PubMed Central). Examples: PLOS ONE, journals in DOAJ.

3. Peer-reviewed journals: Submitted manuscripts are evaluated by independent subject-matter experts before acceptance. This is the cornerstone of scientific quality control. The process: Submit → Editorial check → Peer review (2–3 reviewers) → Revision → Accept/Reject → Publish.

4. Predatory journals: Fraudulent journals that charge APCs without providing genuine peer review. Warning signs: unsolicited email invitations, rapid acceptance (24–48 hrs), vague editorial board, poor website. Detection: use Beall's List, DOAJ whitelist, Think-Check-Submit checklist.

Why does peer review matter?
Peer review is the quality filter of science. Without it, anyone can publish anything. It ensures methods are sound, conclusions are supported by data, and the work advances knowledge. However, it is not perfect — it can miss fraud (Schön scandal) or reject correct but unconventional work (Wegener's continental drift was initially rejected).
The Sokal Affair (1996): A physicist submitted a nonsensical physics paper stuffed with jargon to a cultural studies journal. It was published — exposing weak peer review in that field.
How to spot a predatory journal
Apply the Think-Check-Submit checklist: Can you find the journal's publisher? Is the editorial board real and verifiable? Is it indexed in Scopus, WoS, or DOAJ? Was the acceptance unrealistically fast? If any answer is suspicious, it is likely predatory.
"International Journal of Advanced Science and Technology Studies" — generic name, no verified editors, promises publication in 7 days for USD 150 → classic predatory journal profile.
  • Subscription journal: Requires payment to access (e.g., Nature, Science)
  • Gold OA: Author pays APC; free to read (PLOS ONE)
  • Green OA: Author self-archives in repository (arXiv, PubMed Central)
  • Peer review: Independent expert evaluation before publication — quality control of science
  • Predatory journal: Charges fees, no real peer review, spam invitations List 4 signs
  • Detection: Beall's List, DOAJ whitelist, Think-Check-Submit, COPE/OASPA membership
  • Peer review steps: Submit → Editorial check → 2-3 reviewers → Revision → Decision
3.2   Scientific databases — indexing, citation, physics databases ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

Indexing databases collect and organise metadata (title, author, abstract, keywords) to make articles discoverable. Examples: PubMed, DOAJ, arXiv.

Citation databases track not just articles but also who cited whom — enabling impact metric calculations. The two major ones are:
— Web of Science (WoS): By Clarivate. Selective — high quality threshold. Source of the official Journal Impact Factor (JIF).
— Scopus: By Elsevier. Broader coverage; more non-English journals. Source of CiteScore, SNIP, and SJR.

Discipline-specific physics databases:
— ADS (Astrophysics Data System): NASA-funded. Literature in astronomy, astrophysics, physics.
— NED (NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database): Data on objects outside the Milky Way (galaxies, quasars, AGN).
— SIMBAD: CDS Strasbourg. Objects within the Milky Way (stars, nebulae, clusters).
— VizieR: CDS Strasbourg. Full numerical data tables and catalogues from published papers.
— PubMed: NLM/NCBI. Biomedical and life science literature (biophysics).
— INSPIRE-HEP: High energy physics literature (particle physics, accelerators, CERN).
— ICDD (X-ray database): Powder diffraction data for material identification in condensed matter physics.

Indexing vs citation database — the key difference
An indexing database helps you find papers (discovery tool). A citation database helps you measure the impact of papers and trace the intellectual lineage of ideas (analysis tool). Think of it as: Google for papers (indexing) vs a family tree of citations (citation database).
PubMed tells you "this paper exists and here's what it's about." Scopus tells you "this paper exists, and 347 other papers have cited it, and here's who."
Memory trick for astronomy databases
SIMBAD = Stars and objects INside our galaxy. NED = objects Beyond our galaxy (extragalactic). VizieR = data Tables. ADS = literature search engine linking all three.
Looking up the star Betelgeuse? Use SIMBAD. Looking up galaxy M87 (where the first black hole photo was taken)? Use NED.

  • Scopus: Elsevier, broader, source of CiteScore/SNIP/SJR
  • ADS: NASA — astronomy and astrophysics literature
  • NED: NASA/IPAC — extragalactic objects (beyond Milky Way)
  • SIMBAD: CDS — objects within/near Milky Way
  • VizieR: CDS — numerical catalogues and data tables

3.3   Research metrics — journal & author level ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

Journal-level metrics:
— Impact Factor (IF): Citations in year Y to articles from years (Y-1) and (Y-2), divided by total articles published in those two years. Published by Clarivate via JCR.
— CiteScore: Scopus metric. Citations in 4 years / documents in 4 years.
— SNIP (Source Normalized Impact per Paper): Corrects for different citation habits across fields.
— SJR (SCImago Journal Rank): Weighted citations — a citation from a high-prestige journal counts more (similar to Google PageRank).
— IPP (Impact per Publication): 3-year Scopus metric, similar to IF.

Author-level metrics:
— h-index: A researcher has h-index h if h papers have each been cited ≥ h times. Balances quantity and quality.
— g-index: Top g papers have together received g² citations. More sensitive to highly cited papers than h-index.
— i10-index: Number of papers with ≥ 10 citations. Used by Google Scholar.
— Altmetrics: Non-traditional impact measures — social media mentions, downloads, news articles, policy citations.

Key limitations: Not comparable across fields; review articles inflate IF; disadvantages new journals and early-career researchers; susceptible to self-citation manipulation.

h-index explained simply
Sort your papers from most to least cited. Find the point where the rank equals the citation count. That rank is your h-index. A researcher with h = 20 has 20 papers each cited at least 20 times.
10 papers with citations: 50, 40, 30, 20, 15, 12, 8, 5, 3, 1. At rank 6, citations = 12 ≥ 6 ✓. At rank 7, citations = 8 ≥ 7 ✓. At rank 8, citations = 5 < 8 ✗. So h-index = 7.
Why field comparison of IF is meaningless
Biology papers cite 40–50 references; physics papers cite 20–25. A biology journal will naturally accumulate more citations than a physics journal of equal importance. SNIP corrects for this by normalising against the field's citation potential.
Nature (IF ≈ 65) vs Physical Review Letters (IF ≈ 9) — PRL is one of the most prestigious physics journals in the world, but its IF looks low compared to biology journals. SNIP makes them comparable.
  • IF formula: Citations to (Y-1, Y-2) / Articles in (Y-1, Y-2) — Clarivate/JCR
  • CiteScore: 4-year Scopus metric
  • SNIP: Field-normalised — corrects for citation culture differences
  • SJR: Weighted citations — high-prestige citing journal = more weight
  • h-index: h papers each cited ≥ h times — balances quantity and quality Calculate it
  • g-index: Top g papers together have g² citations — rewards blockbusters
  • i10-index: Papers with ≥10 citations — Google Scholar metric
  • Altmetrics: Social media, news, policy mentions of research
  • IF limitations: Field-dependent, review article bias, new journal bias, manipulation
UNIT IV Scientific Conduct & Publication Ethics 8 hrs
4.1   Research integrity — errors, records, honest reporting ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

Research integrity refers to the adherence to ethical principles and professional standards throughout the research process. Its core elements include: intellectual honesty, transparency, accountability, fairness, and respect for all participants.

Communicating errors:
— Erratum: Minor error introduced by the author (typo, wrong unit). Published as a short notice. Does not change conclusions.
— Corrigendum/Correction: More substantial error. May affect interpretation. Issued by author or publisher.
— Retraction: Complete withdrawal of the paper due to major flaws — data fabrication, duplicate publication, or errors that invalidate the conclusions. The paper remains visible but marked "RETRACTED." Tracked by Retraction Watch.
— Expression of Concern: Interim notice issued when an investigation is ongoing but misconduct is not yet confirmed.

Records to maintain: Raw data, lab notebooks, observation logs, experimental protocols, analysis code, sample records. Must be retained for at least 5–10 years post-publication. Reason: enables reproducibility, protects against misconduct allegations, supports error correction.

Erratum vs Retraction — severity spectrum
Think of it as: Erratum = a spelling mistake in a newspaper. Correction = a factual error that needs a prominent fix. Retraction = the entire story was fabricated and must be unpublished.
The Hwang Woo-suk stem cell scandal (2006): faked embryonic stem cell data led to full retraction of two Nature papers — one of the most high-profile retractions in biology.
Why keep records?
Good record-keeping is your insurance policy. If a result is questioned years later, your raw data and protocols prove you did the experiment correctly. Without records, you cannot defend your work — and suspicion is impossible to disprove.
In 2014, the STAP stem cell paper was published in Nature. When others could not reproduce the results, the authors were asked to provide raw data and lab notebooks. Their absence led to a retraction and one author's tragic death.
  • Intellectual honesty: Accurate reporting, acknowledging limitations, proper attribution
  • Erratum: Minor author error — typo, calculation; does NOT change conclusions
  • Corrigendum: Substantial error — may affect interpretation
  • Retraction: Full withdrawal — fabrication, fraud, or fatal errors Most severe
  • Expression of Concern: Interim notice during ongoing investigation
  • Records to keep: Raw data, protocols, lab notebooks, analysis code, sample logs
  • Retention period: Minimum 5–10 years post-publication
  • Retraction Watch: Database tracking all retracted papers
4.2   Plagiarism — types, detection & avoidance ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

Plagiarism is the act of presenting another person's ideas, words, data, images, or creative work as one's own without proper attribution. It is a form of intellectual theft and academic dishonesty.

Types of plagiarism:
1. Verbatim (direct) plagiarism: Word-for-word copying without quotation marks or citation.
2. Paraphrasing plagiarism: Rewording another's ideas without citing the source.
3. Mosaic plagiarism: Mixing copied phrases from multiple sources without attribution.
4. Self-plagiarism: Reusing one's own previously published work in a new submission without disclosure.
5. Idea plagiarism: Stealing someone's concept, theory, or argument without attribution even if the wording is different.
6. Ghost authorship: Someone else writes the work but the submitter claims authorship.

Detection tools: iThenticate (used by journals), Turnitin (used by universities), PlagScan, Unicheck. These compare submitted text against databases of published papers and websites and generate a similarity report with percentage match.

Avoidance: Proper citation (APA/MLA/Vancouver), paraphrasing with attribution, using reference managers, and ensuring the similarity index remains below 15–20% (institution-specific threshold).

Self-plagiarism — the surprising one
Many students don't realise that reusing your own earlier work without disclosure is also plagiarism. When you publish a paper, copyright often transfers to the journal. Submitting the same content again without permission violates that copyright and misleads readers about the novelty of the work.
A researcher publishes findings in a conference proceedings, then submits the same manuscript to a journal without disclosing the previous publication → self-plagiarism / duplicate publication.
What is an acceptable similarity index?
There is no universal rule, but most universities and journals consider below 15–20% similarity acceptable, provided it is not concentrated in one source. A 5% match that is entirely verbatim from a key reference may be more problematic than 18% spread across many correctly cited sources.
Turnitin flags a thesis at 22% similarity. On investigation, most matched text is from the student's own properly cited references and standard definitions. The actual plagiarism may be near 0%.
  • Plagiarism: Presenting others' work as your own without attribution
  • Verbatim: Word-for-word copy — most obvious form
  • Paraphrasing: Reword without citation — equally unacceptable
  • Mosaic: Mix phrases from multiple sources without attribution
  • Self-plagiarism: Reuse own published work without disclosure
  • iThenticate: Used by journals — compares against published literature Name this tool
  • Turnitin: Used by universities — generates similarity report
  • Acceptable similarity: Usually <15–20% (institution-specific)
  • Avoidance: Cite properly, paraphrase with attribution, use reference manager
4.3   Publication misconduct — salami slicing, COPE, waste disposal ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

Redundant publications are forms of publication misconduct that inflate the literature without adding knowledge:

— Salami slicing: Artificially dividing one coherent study into the smallest possible number of separate publications ("least publishable units") solely to maximise publication count.
— Duplicate publication: Submitting the same manuscript to two journals simultaneously, or publishing the same data in two journals without disclosure.
— Overlapping publications: Publishing partially overlapping datasets in different papers without disclosure.
— Selective reporting: Reporting only statistically significant (positive) results while suppressing negative or null results — causes publication bias in the literature.
— Data misrepresentation: Manipulating graphs, selectively cropping images, or cherry-picking data to support a predetermined conclusion.

COPE (Committee on Publication Ethics): An international body (founded 1997) that provides guidelines, case studies, and flowcharts to help editors and publishers handle allegations of misconduct. Core practices: data integrity, authorship, peer review, conflicts of interest, and handling of misconduct allegations. COPE does NOT investigate cases — it advises editors.

Environmental clearances: Research involving hazardous chemicals, radioactive materials, or biological agents requires prior clearance. Waste must be segregated, labelled, stored safely, and disposed of through licensed agencies per national regulations (Environment Protection Act, India).

Why is salami slicing harmful?
When researchers conduct a systematic review or meta-analysis, they count studies. A salami-sliced project counted as 5 papers inflates the apparent evidence base, biases the meta-analysis, and can mislead clinical practice or policy.
A clinical trial testing a drug on 1,000 patients — results split into: cardiovascular outcomes (Paper 1), respiratory outcomes (Paper 2), renal outcomes (Paper 3). This should have been one paper with subgroup analyses.
COPE flowcharts — what they cover
COPE has specific flowcharts for: suspected plagiarism after publication, redundant publication, authorship disputes, peer reviewer misconduct, data fabrication, and undisclosed conflicts of interest. These are used by journal editors as decision guides.
An editor receives a tip that a published paper was also submitted to another journal. They consult the COPE "Suspected redundant publication — after publication" flowchart, contact both journals, and coordinate a retraction.
  • Salami slicing: Splitting one study into minimum publishable units to inflate count
  • Duplicate publication: Same manuscript submitted to two journals simultaneously
  • Selective reporting: Only publishing positive results → publication bias
  • Data misrepresentation: Cropping images, cherry-picking data, manipulating graphs
  • COPE: Committee on Publication Ethics — provides guidelines and flowcharts Founded 1997
  • COPE does NOT investigate: It advises editors — enforcement is institutional
  • COPE core practices: Data integrity, authorship, peer review, conflicts of interest
  • Hazardous waste: Must be segregated, labelled, stored safely, disposed via licensed agencies

5.1   Structure of a research paper ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

A standard research paper follows the IMRaD structure (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion):

1. Title: Concise, specific, contains key terms for indexing. Typically 10–15 words.
2. Abstract: 150–250 words, self-contained summary covering: background, objective, methods, key results, and conclusion. No citations, no abbreviations, written in past tense.
3. Introduction: Background context, review of relevant literature, identification of the research gap, objectives and hypothesis of the study. Answers: "Why did you do this?"
4. Methodology: Materials, instruments, experimental design, data collection procedures, statistical methods. Must be written in sufficient detail to allow replication. Answers: "How did you do it?"
5. Results: Objective presentation of data using text, tables, and figures. No interpretation — just what was found. Answers: "What did you find?"
6. Discussion: Interpretation of results, comparison with existing literature, limitations of the study, implications. Answers: "What does it mean?"
7. Conclusion: Summary of findings, direct answer to the research question, future scope.
8. References: All cited sources in a consistent format (APA/MLA/Vancouver).

IMRaD — why this structure?
IMRaD evolved because it mirrors the scientific thinking process: you frame a question (Introduction), design a way to answer it (Methods), observe what happens (Results), and make sense of what you found (Discussion). It also allows a busy reader to read only the section they need.
A physicist reading for methodology can jump directly to Methods. A reviewer checking statistical rigour goes to Results. A student writing a review reads only the Introduction and Discussion of 50 papers.
Results vs Discussion — the most confused sections
Results section = a factual report: "The mean temperature increased by 3.2°C ± 0.4." No interpretation, no opinion. Discussion = interpretation: "The observed 3.2°C increase is consistent with the thermal runaway model proposed by [Smith, 2019], suggesting that..."
WRONG in Results: "The drug worked well." RIGHT in Results: "The treatment group showed a 40% reduction in symptom score (p = 0.003)." The word "worked" is an interpretation — it belongs in Discussion.
  • IMRaD: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion — standard structure
  • Abstract: 150–250 words; background + objective + methods + results + conclusion
  • Abstract rules: No citations, no abbreviations, no figures, past tense for methods/results
  • Introduction: Background → gap → objective → hypothesis
  • Methods: Detailed enough to be reproducible by others
  • Results: Data only — no interpretation Common exam trap
  • Discussion: Interpretation, comparison with literature, limitations
  • Conclusion: Summary of findings + future scope
  • Title: Concise, specific, 10–15 words, contains indexing keywords

Referencing formats:
— APA (American Psychological Association): Author-date in-text citation. Used in social sciences, psychology. Format: (Author, Year). End reference: Author, A. A. (Year). Title (edition). Publisher.
— MLA (Modern Language Association): Author-page in-text citation. Used in humanities. Format: (Author Page). End reference: Author Last, First. Title. Publisher, Year.
— Vancouver (numbered): In-text: [1], [2]. Used in medicine and physics. References listed in order of citation.

Graphical software:
— GnuPlot: Free, open-source, command-line tool. Script-driven, publication-quality output (eps, pdf, png). Best for automation and reproducibility.
— Origin (OriginLab): Paid, GUI-based. Point-and-click interface. Supports curve fitting and batch analysis. Most commonly used in physics labs.
— MagicPlot: Lightweight, GUI. Free student version. Good for curve fitting.

Presentation tools:
— Beamer (LaTeX): Creates PDF slides. Superior for complex equations. Platform-independent, version-controllable, reproducible. Structure: \begin{frame}...\end{frame} per slide.

APA vs MLA — the key distinction
APA emphasises the year because in social sciences, recency matters — a 1990 psychology theory may have been superseded. MLA emphasises the author because in humanities, the exact words and the writer's authority matter more than when it was written.
APA: (Kothari, 2008, p. 45) — year is prominent. MLA: (Kothari 45) — page number is prominent, year goes in Works Cited at the end.
Why Beamer over PowerPoint for science?
A physics presentation with 20 equations would look terrible in PowerPoint but beautiful in Beamer. More importantly, the Beamer source file is a plain text .tex file — it can be version-controlled with Git, shared easily, and generates a PDF that looks identical on every computer, with no "fonts not embedded" surprises.
Every CERN, IIT, and NASA presentation you have seen that uses a clean, consistent, equation-heavy PDF format is almost certainly made with Beamer.

  • MLA: Author-page — (Kothari 45) — humanities

UNIT VI Intellectual Property Rights & Research Funding 4 hrs
6.1   Intellectual property — patent, copyright, trademark ▼
Exam answer
Concept + example
Point-wise
Write this on your answer sheet

Intellectual Property (IP) refers to creations of the mind protected by law. The three main types relevant to research are:

1. Patent: Grants exclusive rights to an inventor over a novel invention for a period of 20 years from the filing date. Requirements: Novelty (not previously disclosed), Non-obviousness (inventive step — not obvious to a skilled person), and Industrial applicability (capable of practical use). The inventor must fully disclose the invention (enablement). Not patentable: scientific theories, mathematical methods, natural phenomena, methods of medical treatment (Section 3, Indian Patents Act 1970).

2. Copyright: Protects original creative expression — books, research papers, software code, musical compositions, artworks. Arises automatically on creation (no registration required). Duration: Life of the author + 60 years (India). Copyright is owned by the author unless assigned to a publisher.

3. Trademark: Protects brand identity — name, logo, symbol, slogan, or distinctive colour combination. Must be registered to be enforceable. Renewable indefinitely (every 10 years in India).

Trade secret: Confidential business information protected through secrecy (no registration). No expiry, but no legal protection if the information is independently discovered or leaked.

Patent — the innovation trade-off
A patent is a social contract: the inventor discloses how the invention works (enabling others to learn and build upon it after expiry) in exchange for a 20-year monopoly. If the inventor did NOT patent it, they might keep it secret indefinitely as a trade secret — but science progresses slower. The patent system forces disclosure.
When Pfizer's patent on Lipitor (atorvastatin) expired in 2011, generic manufacturers could legally produce the same drug at a fraction of the cost — saving patients billions of dollars globally.
Copyright vs Patent — the crucial difference for researchers
Copyright protects how an idea is expressed — not the idea itself. A patent protects the idea (the invention). When you publish a paper, the words and structure are copyrighted (usually transferred to the publisher), but the scientific findings and methods within it cannot be copyrighted — they can be reproduced and built upon (though a new patentable application of them could be patented).
Einstein's theory of relativity — the equations and ideas are not copyrightable (scientific facts). But his original 1905 paper (the specific text) is copyrighted. Anyone can use E = mc² but cannot republish his original paper without permission.
  • Patent: 20 years, novel + non-obvious + industrially applicable, requires disclosure
  • Patent requirements: Novelty, Non-obviousness (inventive step), Industrial applicability
  • Not patentable: Scientific theories, maths, natural phenomena, medical treatment methods
  • Copyright: Automatic, no registration, life + 60 years (India), protects expression
  • Trademark: Must register, renewable indefinitely, protects brand identity
  • Trade secret: No registration, no expiry, but no protection if leaked 4th type
  • Key distinction: Patent = protects invention; Copyright = protects expression; Trademark = protects identity
  • Indian Patents Act: 1970; Section 3 lists non-patentable subjects

Research funding agencies provide financial support for scientific investigation through grants and fellowships. In India, the key agencies are:

1. DST-SERB (Science and Engineering Research Board): Under Dept. of Science & Technology. Funds basic and applied research across all science disciplines. Key schemes: CRG (Core Research Grant — for established researchers), SRG (Startup Research Grant — for new faculty ≤ 3 years), MATRICS (mathematical sciences), J C Bose National Fellowship (for outstanding scientists), NPDF (National Post-Doctoral Fellowship).

2. UGC (University Grants Commission): Funds academic research in universities and colleges. Key schemes: Major Research Projects (MRP), Minor Research Projects, UGC-JRF/SRF (fellowships for PhD students via NET exam).

3. CSIR (Council of Scientific and Industrial Research): India's largest publicly funded R&D organisation. Provides extramural grants and JRF/SRF fellowships (via CSIR-NET).

4. BRNS (Board of Research in Nuclear Sciences): Under DAE (Dept. of Atomic Energy). Funds research relevant to nuclear sciences, radiation, materials science.

5. DRDO (Defence Research and Development Organisation): Funds research with strategic and defence applications.

International fellowships: Fulbright (US-India), Newton-Bhabha (UK-India), Marie Curie (European Union), DST-NSF (Indo-US), DST-DFG (Indo-German).

Why do funding agencies matter for research?
Without external funding, most research cannot happen — equipment, salaries, travel, and publication costs require money. Funding agencies shape the direction of science by deciding what gets funded. They also enforce research integrity — most require data management plans, ethical clearances, and periodic progress reports.
The Chandrayaan missions were funded by ISRO under DST. Without government funding, no private entity in India could have financed planetary exploration.
SERB vs UGC — key comparison
SERB is about research output — it funds scientists to conduct experiments and publish. UGC is about institutions — it funds universities to build research infrastructure and supports students through fellowships. A professor might receive a SERB-CRG grant to run a project AND teach students funded by UGC-JRF fellowships.
Prof. X at IIT gets a SERB-CRG grant of ₹50 lakhs to study 2D materials, while two of her PhD students are supported by CSIR-JRF fellowships — two separate agencies, complementary roles.
  • SERB: Under DST; CRG, SRG, MATRICS, J C Bose Fellowship, NPDF
  • UGC: Universities/colleges; MRP, Minor RP, JRF/SRF (via NET)
  • CSIR: Largest public R&D; extramural grants + JRF/SRF (via CSIR-NET)
  • BRNS: Under DAE — nuclear, radiation, materials science research
  • DRDO: Defence-relevant research Name all 5
  • CRG: For established researchers; SRG: for new faculty ≤ 3 years
  • CSIR-NET vs UGC-NET: Both qualify for JRF/SRF but under different agencies
  • International: Fulbright (US), Newton-Bhabha (UK), Marie Curie (EU)
  • Key difference SERB vs UGC: SERB = research output; UGC = institutional development
