1. Amorphous vs Crystalline Solids
Concept ⌄
Definition & Key Differences
Crystalline solids: atoms arranged in a perfectly regular, periodic 3D pattern. Exhibit long-range order. Examples: NaCl, diamond, quartz, metals.
Amorphous solids: atoms arranged randomly, no long-range periodicity. Only short-range order exists. Examples: glass, rubber, wax, plastics.

2. Lattice, Basis & Unit Cell
Core ⌄
Lattice
A crystal lattice is an infinite array of points in 3D space, where every point has an identical environment. It describes the geometric arrangement.
Lattice translation vectors: a, b, c are three non-coplanar vectors. Any lattice point is reached by: R = n₁a + n₂b + n₃c where n₁, n₂, n₃ are integers.
Basis
A basis is the group of atoms (or molecules) associated with each lattice point.
Key relation: Crystal Structure = Lattice + Basis
Example: NaCl lattice is FCC, with basis = one Na⁺ + one Cl⁻ ion.
Unit Cell
The unit cell is the smallest volume that, when repeated by translation, generates the entire crystal.
Primitive unit cell: contains exactly 1 lattice point. Volume = a·(b×c).
Non-primitive cell: contains more than 1 lattice point (BCC=2, FCC=4).
Wigner-Seitz cell: primitive cell formed by drawing perpendicular bisectors between a lattice point and all its neighbors.
Bravais Lattices
There are 14 Bravais lattices in 3D, grouped under 7 crystal systems: Cubic, Tetragonal, Orthorhombic, Monoclinic, Triclinic, Trigonal, Hexagonal.
Cubic system has 3 lattices: SC (Simple Cubic), BCC, FCC.

3. Miller Indices
Exam Favourite ⌄
Definition
Miller indices (hkl) are a set of integers used to describe the orientation of crystal planes and directions.
Steps to Find Miller Indices
Step-by-step Method (write this in exam)
Find where the plane intercepts the three crystal axes: x-intercept = p, y-intercept = q, z-intercept = r (in units of a, b, c).
Take the reciprocals: 1/p, 1/q, 1/r.
Clear fractions by multiplying with the LCM to get three smallest integers.
Enclose in parentheses: (hkl). Negative value → put bar on top: (h̄kl).
Special Cases
Plane parallel to an axis → intercept = ∞ → Miller index = 0.
(100): plane cuts x-axis, parallel to y and z.
(110): plane cuts x and y, parallel to z.
(111): plane cuts all three axes equally.
{hkl} denotes a family of planes; [hkl] denotes a direction; ⟨hkl⟩ denotes a family of directions.
Interplanar Spacing (Cubic System)
d_hkl = a / √(h² + k² + l²)
where a = lattice parameter, (h, k, l) = Miller indices
Numerical problems: given a plane that cuts axes at (2, 3, 1) → reciprocals = (1/2, 1/3, 1) → LCM=6 → (3, 2, 6). Practice 5–6 such problems before the exam.
4. Reciprocal Lattice
Core ⌄
Concept
The reciprocal lattice is a mathematical construct in k-space (momentum space) derived from the direct lattice.
Every point in the reciprocal lattice corresponds to a set of parallel planes (hkl) in the direct lattice.
Distance of reciprocal lattice point from origin = 1/d_hkl.
Reciprocal Lattice Vectors
a* = 2π(b × c) / V
b* = 2π(c × a) / V
c* = 2π(a × b) / V

where V = a·(b × c) = volume of unit cell

Orthogonality: a·a* = 2π, a·b* = 0, a·c* = 0
Properties
The reciprocal of the reciprocal lattice is the original direct lattice.
BCC direct lattice ↔ FCC reciprocal lattice, and vice versa.
Diffraction condition: Δk = G (where G is a reciprocal lattice vector). This is the Laue condition.
5. Brillouin Zones & Ewald Construction
Important ⌄
Brillouin Zones
A Brillouin zone is defined as a primitive Wigner-Seitz cell developed inside the reciprocal lattice space.
The boundaries of these zones reflect the locations where moving waves meet conditions for Bragg reflection.
First Brillouin Zone: The smallest enclosed volume around the reciprocal lattice origin boundary lines. It marks the fundamental region for calculating electronic bands.
Ewald Sphere Construction
A geometric framework used to determine which reciprocal lattice points will yield visible diffraction for a given X-ray wavelength.
Method: Draw a vector of length 1/λ matching the incoming beam direction to end exactly at the reciprocal origin. Trace a sphere of radius 1/λ from the start of that vector. Everywhere the sphere surface intersects a reciprocal lattice point, a valid diffraction beam is produced.
Bragg's Law of Diffraction
2d sin(θ) = nλ

where d = interplanar spacing, θ = Bragg angle, λ = incident wave wavelength, n = order of reflection

1. Monoatomic vs Diatomic Linear Lattice
Core ⌄
Monoatomic Lattice Dynamics
Models a 1D line of identical atoms of mass M separated by uniform spacing a, bound together by spring constant C.
Yields exactly one Acoustic branch. At tiny wavevectors (k → 0), frequency scales linearly with wavevector (ω ∝ k), matching normal sound waves.
Diatomic Lattice Dynamics
Models a alternating chain of two differing masses (M₁ and M₂) with equal spring connection parameters.
Splits into two distinct movement paths: Acoustic Branch (atoms swing together in sync) and Optical Branch (atoms bounce in opposite paths against one another).
Diatomic Dispersion Relations at Zone Boundaries
Acoustic Max: ω² = 2C / M₁ (assuming M₁ > M₂)
Optical Min: ω² = 2C / M₂
Forbidden Band Gap Span: Δω = √(2C/M₂) - √(2C/M₁)
When asked to contrast branches, sketch the frequency ω vs wavevector k plot. Show the acoustic branch starting at origin, the optical branch plateauing higher up, and label the forbidden gap between them explicitly.
2. Einstein vs Debye Models of Specific Heat
Exam Favourite ⌄
Background Rule
Classical physics (Dulong-Petit law) states specific heat is constant at 3R. This works at high temperatures but completely fails near absolute zero, where experimental heat capacity drops towards 0.
Model Interpretations
Einstein Model: Assumes all lattice atoms bounce independently at a single fixed frequency ω_E. It correctly predicts specific heat falls at low temperatures, but it drops off too rapidly (exponentially) compared to reality.
Debye Model: Treats lattice steps as continuous elastic sound waves instead. Assumes a range of frequencies up to a hard cut-off limit (Debye frequency ω_D). Matches experiments perfectly near absolute zero.
Debye T³ Law (Low Temperature Limit)
C_v = (12π⁴ / 5) · R · (T / θ_D)³

where θ_D = Debye Temperature parameter

1. Kronig-Penney Model & Band Formation
Core ⌄
Concept Definition
Solves the Schrödinger equation for an electron traveling through a periodic square potential well path to simulate the lattice ion cores.
Demonstrates that electron energy states break up into allowed energy bands and forbidden energy gaps due to wave reflection conditions.
Kronig-Penney Condition Equation
P · (sin(αa) / αa) + cos(αa) = cos(ka)

where P = scattering power barrier strength, α = term related to energy E, a = lattice period span
Exam Execution Checklist
State that Bloch's function ψ(x) = u(x)e^{ikx} is applied because the potential is periodic.
Write the condition equation shown above.
Explain that since the right side cos(ka) must fall between -1 and +1, any energy value forcing the left side outside those boundaries becomes a forbidden gap.
2. Concept of Effective Mass
Formula ⌄
Definition and Origin
An electron inside a crystal moves under the influence of both an external applied field and internal periodic crystal forces.
Instead of accounting for complex internal forces directly, we treat the electron as a free particle with an adjusted mass called the effective mass (m*).
Effective mass depends directly on the curvature of the Energy-momentum (E-k) band diagram. At the base of a band, it is positive; near the top of a band, curvature flips negative, representing a hole.
Effective Mass Formula
m* = ħ² / (d²E / dk²)

where ħ = reduced Planck constant, d²E/dk² = band curvature parameter

2. Weiss Theory & Hysteresis Loop
Important ⌄
Domain Framework
Weiss proposed that ferromagnets are divided into tiny regions called domains. Inside each domain, individual atomic spins are perfectly aligned spontaneously due to internal exchange forces.
When an external magnetic field is applied, these domains respond by growing larger or rotating their alignment vector to point along the external field line.
Hysteresis Curve Terms
Retentivity (Residual Magnetism): The amount of magnetic induction remaining inside the material when the external driving field drops back to zero.
Coercivity: The reverse magnetic field strength needed to strip away the remaining internal magnetization completely.
When sketching the Hysteresis loop (B vs H curve), label the saturation point, retentivity on the vertical axis, and coercivity on the horizontal axis. The area enclosed by the loop directly matches the thermal energy lost per cycle.
UNIT V
Dielectric Properties

Polarization & Local Fields

Dielectric constant · Polarization mechanisms · Local internal field · Clausius-Mossotti relation · Ferroelectricity
1. Polarization Mechanisms
Core ⌄
The Four Basic Processes
Electronic Polarization: The external field displaces the negatively charged electron cloud relative to the positive nucleus. Occurs in all materials and is independent of temperature.
Ionic Polarization: The field physically shifts positive and negative ions in opposite directions within an ionic crystal grid (like NaCl). Temperature independent.
Orientational (Dipolar) Polarization: Pre-existing permanent molecular dipoles rotate to line up with the applied field direction. Heavily dependent on temperature, dropping as thermal agitation increases (∝ 1/T).
Space Charge Polarization: Mobile charge carriers trap themselves at structural crystal defect borders or grain boundaries. Occurs only at low frequencies.
2. Lorentz Local Field & Clausius-Mossotti Equation
Formula ⌄
Lorentz Internal Field
The actual electric field seen by an individual atom inside a dielectric material is not just the external applied field. It includes fields generated by surrounding polarized dipoles.
Lorentz Calculation: E_local = E + P / (3ε₀), where E is macroscopic field, P is polarization density, and ε₀ is vacuum permittivity.
Clausius-Mossotti Relation
(ε_r - 1) / (ε_r + 2) = (N · α) / (3ε₀)

where ε_r = relative dielectric constant, N = number of atoms per volume unit, α = total atomic polarizability
The Clausius-Mossotti relation links a macroscopic property (dielectric constant ε_r) to a microscopic property (atomic polarizability α). Expect proofs or simple numerical substitutions for this relation on exam papers.
